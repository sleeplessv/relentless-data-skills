# Research: what review rounds and model tiers buy in orchestrated coding

Dated 2026-11-16. This note records the outside evidence behind two changes to this repo's orchestration skills: running reviews as one parallel wave with a capped fix loop, and assigning model classes by role with escalation on a failed check. It asks four questions. Can an agent tell when it needs a stronger model? What does a further review pass add, and what does it cost? What does a multi-agent pipeline cost against a single agent? When is a stronger model or a higher effort level worth paying for? The evidence is mostly one vendor measuring its own product, single runs, or single preprints, and each paragraph says which. It is a record of what the evidence supports, not a literature survey. The repo's own run data is in [orchestrated-run-measurements.md](orchestrated-run-measurements.md).

Method: every figure and quoted phrase below was re-read on 2026-11-16 in the text of the linked page, fetched raw and not through a summarising reader. For arXiv papers the abstract page and the HTML full text were both read. Figures that appear only in a chart image were not carried. Model names are the ones each source used. Several Anthropic links redirect (`claude.com/blog/...` to `claude.com/resources/articles/...`, `anthropic.com/engineering/...` to `anthropic.com/research/...`); the links below are the addresses that were requested and resolved.

## Agents do not reliably know when they are failing

A February 2026 preprint asked three coding agents to estimate their own success on 100 SWE-bench Pro tasks. GPT-5.2-Codex, Gemini 3 Pro and Claude Opus 4.5 actually solved 35%, 22% and 27%. After finishing, they predicted 73%, 77% and 61%. Their post-execution estimates separated passes from failures with AUROC 0.58, 0.51 and 0.55, where 0.5 is chance. The paper reports that "62% of predictions on failing instances are overconfident (predicted ≥ 0.7), while only 11% of predictions on passing instances are underconfident", a ratio it gives as 5.5 times. Asking the agent to hunt for bugs in its own patch improved calibration (expected calibration error fell from 0.42 to 0.30 for GPT and from 0.37 to 0.24 for Claude) and roughly doubled the cost of the estimate (23.4 steps at $0.52 per instance against 12.7 at $0.23). In a 25-task ablation on who judges the patch, every condition stayed overconfident. This is one preprint, with as few as 22 passing examples for one model and wide confidence intervals. [Kaddour et al., arXiv 2602.06948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06948).

A second preprint, across several task types, reaches the same direction with a qualification: "All LLMs we tested are overconfident, but most predict their success with better-than-random discriminatory power". On multi-step SWE-Bench Verified tasks it found that "all Claude Sonnet models became more overconfident (on average) as they progressed through the tasks", and that reasoning models did no better than non-reasoning ones at this. [Barkan et al., arXiv 2512.24661](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24661).

Cognition reports the practical version. It tried a weaker primary model that could call a stronger one, and found that "the quality ceiling was set by the primary, and the primary wasn't strong enough", because the gap showed up in "knowing when to escalate, knowing what to ask". Its mitigations were unconditional: "always make at least one call to the smart agent", and always calling it for merge conflicts. It calls the weaker-primary arrangement "still an open problem". This is a vendor's account of its own product with no numbers attached. [Cognition, Multi-Agents: What's Actually Working](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working).

## The advisor tool's documented guidance uses fixed checkpoints, and its measured gains are small

Anthropic's advisor tool lets a cheaper executor model consult a stronger model inside one request. It is in beta (header `advisor-tool-2026-03-01`). The documentation states that "the executor model determines when to call it, like any other tool", that the advisor "receives the executor's full transcript", that it "runs without tools and without context management", and that only its advice text returns. The advisor must be at least as capable as the executor. Its output is "typically 400 to 700 text tokens, or 1,400 to 1,800 tokens total including thinking", billed at the advisor model's rates and reported separately from the top-level totals. `max_uses` caps calls per turn, and the tool "has no built-in conversation-level cap". [Advisor tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/advisor-tool).

The suggested system prompt on that page does not rely on the executor feeling stuck. It tells the executor to call the advisor before substantive work, and on longer tasks "at least once before committing to an approach and once before declaring done", on the grounds that "the advisor adds most of its value on the first call, before the approach crystallizes". Being stuck is one trigger in a list, not the main one. For Haiku the page offers a hard rule that the first state-changing action must follow an advisor call: "This is a checkpoint, not a difficulty judgment. It applies to one-line edits too." The launch post frames it differently ("When the executor hits a decision it can't reasonably solve, it consults Opus for guidance"), so the two Anthropic pages do not agree on emphasis. [Advisor tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/advisor-tool), [The advisor strategy](https://claude.com/blog/the-advisor-strategy).

The same page reports Anthropic's internal measurements of that guidance, all on unnamed internal benchmarks:

- The Haiku hard rule "raised Haiku pass rates by roughly 7.5 percentage points over the built-in default" on a coding benchmark. A close variant "cost roughly 4 percentage points of accuracy" on a browse-comprehension benchmark (n = 1,266), which is why the published rule now exempts read-only work.
- A reminder before the second assistant turn raised Haiku 4.5 pass rates "by roughly 7 percentage points", had "no measurable effect" on Sonnet, and slightly lowered Opus pass rates. Where the first call would naturally have come at turn 7 or later, the early nudge "correlated with a 3 to 4 percentage-point task-performance drop".
- A checkpoint rule for Opus "raised pass rates on under-calling tasks by roughly 7 to 10 percentage points but caused Opus to over-call", and "The net effect was roughly flat on a mixed workload."
- Capping advisor `max_tokens` at 2048 "reduced mean advisor output by roughly 7x" with "no detectable quality degradation" (n = 40 per configuration).

The page's own caveat is "Results are task-dependent. Evaluate on your own workload." [Advisor tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/advisor-tool).

The launch post (9 April 2026) gives two headline results, both Anthropic's own. "Sonnet with Opus as an advisor showed a 2.7 percentage point increase on SWE-bench Multilingual over Sonnet alone, while reducing cost per agentic task by 11.9%". The footnote shows the two arms differed in more than the advisor: Sonnet alone used adaptive thinking, and the advisor arm used the suggested coding prompt with thinking off. On BrowseComp, Haiku with an Opus advisor scored 41.2% against 19.7% alone, and "trails Sonnet solo by 29% in score but costs 85% less per task". [The advisor strategy](https://claude.com/blog/the-advisor-strategy).

In Claude Code the advisor is experimental. "Subagents inherit the configured advisor and apply the same pairing check against their own model." The timing "is model-driven rather than rule-based", and "There is no setting to cap or force advisor calls". Each call reprocesses the whole transcript: "The advisor model's own read of the conversation is not cached." A skill therefore cannot bound advisor spend through configuration, only through instructions. [Claude Code advisor](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/advisor.md). In Managed Agents the rule is the opposite for subagents: "Only the primary thread can consult the advisor". [Managed Agents multiagent orchestration](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/managed-agents/multiagent-orchestration).

## A strong planner over cheaper workers is widely shipped, and measured only in vendor single runs

Anthropic's research system used Claude Opus 4 as lead and Claude Sonnet 4 subagents, and "outperformed single-agent Claude Opus 4 by 90.2% on our internal research eval". That is a research task on an internal evaluation, and the same post says "most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research". [How we built our multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system).

Cursor's July 2026 post is the closest thing to a costed coding result. A swarm with an Opus 4.8 planner and Composer 2.5 workers cost $1,339 on one large build, against $10,565 for GPT-5.5 in every role. Worker spend was $411 against $9,373. Workers used "at least 69% of the tokens, and over 90% in most" configurations, while the planner took "a small fraction of the tokens but roughly two-thirds of the cost". Its reading is "Few moments in a large task genuinely require frontier intelligence". The limits are stated in the post: the full matrix of pairings was not run, the solo runs were graded informally ("we draw no conclusions about their quality here"), and a second hybrid run with a planner at roughly twice the per-token price came out more expensive overall because its workers used several times as many tokens. The post reports no repeat runs. [Cursor, Agent swarms and the new model economics](https://cursor.com/blog/agent-swarm-model-economics).

Aider's two benchmark posts show the split helping on its own edit benchmark and not always. An o1-preview architect with a cheaper editor scored 85.0% against 79.7% for o1-preview alone. Pairing Sonnet with itself scored 80.5% against 77.4%. [Aider, Separating code reasoning and editing](https://aider.chat/2024/09/26/architect.html). On the later polyglot benchmark, R1 as architect with Sonnet as editor scored 64.0% at $13.29, against o1 alone at 61.7% and $186.5, R1 alone at 56.9% and $5.42, and Sonnet alone at 51.6% and $14.41. The same post notes that "o1 paired with Sonnet didn't produce better results than just using o1 alone." [Aider, R1+Sonnet](https://aider.chat/2025/01/24/r1-sonnet.html).

One preprint points the other way. On a browsing benchmark, Kim et al. tested mixed-capability teams and report that "configurations with high-capability sub-agents outperform those with high-capability orchestrators across all model families". The differences are a few points, the task is browsing and not coding, and the paper's figure caption and table disagree on the baseline. It is enough to stop the planner-matters-most claim being treated as settled. [Kim et al., arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296).

Two facts about the harness matter for a skill that wants tiers. "By default, a subagent you define runs on the same model as the conversation that starts it", so a cheaper worker has to be requested. [Claude Code subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents.md). The cost guidance says "For simple subagent tasks, specify `model: haiku`", and the built-in `opusplan` setting "uses `opus` during plan mode, then switches to `sonnet` for execution". [Claude Code costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs.md), [Claude Code model configuration](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/model-config.md). Subagents also "get five minutes even on a subscription" for prompt-cache lifetime, so a worker resumed after a long gap pays for its context again. [Claude Code prompt caching](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/prompt-caching.md).

## Role pipelines cost multiples of a single agent, and verification is the handoff that pays

Anthropic's January 2026 guidance is the most direct statement on plan, implement and review pipelines. It describes teams that built "separate agents for planning, execution, review, and iteration, only to discover that they suffered from lost context at each handoff and spent more tokens coordinating than executing", and states: "In our testing, multi-agent implementations typically use 3-10x more tokens than single-agent approaches for equivalent tasks." It adds that "In one experiment with agents specialized by software development role (planner, implementer, tester, reviewer), the subagents spent more tokens on coordination than on actual work." It calls splitting by role "Problem-centric decomposition (often counterproductive)" and splitting by what context each piece needs "Context-centric decomposition (usually effective)". These are Anthropic's own tests, described without a benchmark or sample size. [Building multi-agent systems: when and how to use them](https://claude.com/blog/building-multi-agent-systems-when-and-how-to-use-them).

The same post singles out one pattern: "One multi-agent pattern that consistently works well across domains is the verification subagent", because "Verification requires minimal context transfer by nature" and "The verifier does not need to understand why the artifact was built as it was." It also names the failure to guard against: "The verifier runs one or two tests, observes them pass, and declares success." [Building multi-agent systems: when and how to use them](https://claude.com/blog/building-multi-agent-systems-when-and-how-to-use-them).

Other multiples from Anthropic, each its own measurement: agents use "about 4× more tokens than chat interactions, and multi-agent systems use about 15× more tokens than chats". [How we built our multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system). In Claude Code, "Agent teams use approximately 7x more tokens than standard sessions when teammates run in plan mode". [Claude Code costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs.md). A three-agent harness for a long build cost $200 over 6 hours against $9 over 20 minutes for a solo run of the same prompt, with the note that "It is worth the cost when the task sits beyond what the current model does reliably solo." In a later run of that harness the three QA rounds cost $10.39 of a $124.70 total. [Harness design for long-running apps](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps).

Kim et al. (preprint, version 3 of April 2026, 260 configurations) is the only controlled comparison found. Relative to a single agent, multi-agent results ranged from +80.8% to -70.0% by task, and sequential planning tasks lost 39% to 70%. Token overhead was 58% for independent agents, 263% decentralized, 285% centralized and 515% hybrid. Success per 1,000 tokens was 67.7 for the single agent against 42.4, 23.9, 21.5 and 13.6. Returns turned negative once the single agent already exceeded about 45% accuracy. On SWE-bench Verified every multi-agent variant scored slightly below the single agent: hybrid -2.1%, centralized -3.1%, decentralized -5.4%, independent -14.9%. That coding result rests on 20 instances per configuration. [Kim et al., arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296).

A small audit of ChatDev found its code review stage took 59.4% of all tokens across 30 tasks. The authors call the result preliminary, and it concerns one framework. [Tokenomics, arXiv 2601.14470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14470).

## A further review pass without new information adds little, and later rounds add less

Self-review with no external signal can make answers worse. With GPT-4 on GSM8K, accuracy went 95.5, 91.5, 89.0 over one, three and five calls of self-correction. In the same paper's debate test on GSM8K, multi-agent debate with nine responses scored 83.0 against 88.2 for majority voting over nine independent answers. [Huang et al., arXiv 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798). For code, self-repair gained little once the extra samples were counted. For GPT-4 on APPS, one split of the sample budget between first attempts and repairs gave 1.05 times the pass rate of spending the same budget without repair, and another split gave 0.97 times. Feedback from a stronger source helped: GPT-3.5 went from 4.7% to 11.5% with GPT-4's feedback, and GPT-4's repair success went from 33.3% to 52.6% when a human programmer wrote the feedback. [Olausson et al., arXiv 2306.09896](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896).

Gains flatten by round. Self-Refine's code optimisation score went 22.0, 27.0, 27.9, 28.8 over iterations zero to three, so the first round bought 5.0 points and the next two bought 1.8 together. [Madaan et al., arXiv 2303.17651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651).

A single-author preprint tested review conditions on 30 artifacts (code modules, technical tutorials and presentation scripts) with 150 planted errors over 900 sessions. A fresh session of the same model scored F1 28.6% and review inside the authoring session 27.1%, a difference that was not significant. A second pass in the same session scored 21.7%, the only condition significantly different from the fresh-session baseline, and it was worse. One fresh same-model review plus one review by a different model reached 56.7%, against 42.7% for two same-model reviews (adjusted p = .006). The artifacts and their annotations are in Korean, only a third are code, and the paper says model capability and model identity are confounded. [Song, arXiv 2610.01471](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01471).

Anthropic's account of its own code review is the strongest evidence for a cap. "One CLI PR went through more than fifty rounds of review and fixes overnight before its author stopped it", and when the team examined what later rounds flagged, "about 59% of it was code that had been there since the reviewer's first pass". It concedes that "We'd gotten used to running several rounds by default, since each pass catches something new. This can be worthwhile on a risky change, but not on every PR." It made four changes: "A round cap. After three rounds, the reviewer asks the author to step back and reconsider the approach."; "Diff-only re-review after the first few passes."; "Flag the class of bug, so the author fixes every instance, not one line."; and "No repeats of findings already posted." The chart of results (read from its text label) shows one-round passes rising from 65% to 78%, changes needing five or more rounds falling from 8.9% to 1.5%, and mean reviews to pass falling from 2.08 to 1.32. The post also explains why noise is costlier here than in human review: "A person can wave off a nit in seconds. An agent fixes it, which means another commit and another round of review." This is one team's before-and-after on its own traffic, not a controlled trial. [How the Claude Code team does code review](https://claude.dev/blog/solving-code-review-at-anthropic/).

Cognition reports the same loop in its product: "Often the system will loop through multiple code-review cycles, finding new bugs each time (which isn't always great since it can take a while)", and says filtering findings "is key to preventing looping". [Cognition, Multi-Agents: What's Actually Working](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working).

Elaborate agent loops did not beat retrying against a check. On HumanEval, a GPT-4 baseline scored 89.6 at $1.93, retrying up to five times on failing provided tests scored 92.0 at $2.51, Reflexion scored 87.8 at $3.90 and LATS 88.0 at $134.50. [Kapoor et al., arXiv 2407.01502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502).

## Reviewers reject correct code, and a reviewer that rewrites can break working code

Jin and Chen measured how often a model, shown correct code and its specification, recognises it as correct. On HumanEval that rate fell as the review prompt asked for more: GPT-4o 52.4% with a direct judgment, 22.0% when asked to explain, 11.0% when asked to judge, explain and repair. Claude 3.5 Sonnet went 78.0%, 69.5%, 67.0%, and Gemini 2.0 Flash 59.1%, 56.7%, 53.0%. A short peer-reviewed paper on older models, and the clearest evidence that asking a reviewer for more produces more false alarms. [Jin and Chen, arXiv 2508.12358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.12358).

Xiang et al. had one agent write a solution and a second review and return the final program, on 116 LiveCodeBench tasks, without the reviewer being able to run tests.

| Author, reviewer | Pass rate | Cost per task | Fixes | Regressions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Opus 4.7, none | 91.4% | $0.226 | | |
| Claude, reviewed by Codex GPT-5.5 | 82.8% | $0.382 | 3 | 13 |
| Claude, reviewed by itself | 91.4% | $0.389 | 3 | 3 |
| Codex GPT-5.5, none | 71.6% | $0.190 | | |
| Codex, reviewed by Claude | 89.7% | $0.443 | 26 | 5 |
| Codex, reviewed by itself | 84.5% | $0.312 | 21 | 6 |

A weaker reviewer with edit rights lowered the stronger author's pass rate by 8.6 points while raising cost by about 70%. A stronger reviewer raised the weaker author's by 18.1 points. Self-review of the stronger author changed nothing net. The paper states that the direct contrast between the two cross-review orders "does not survive BH correction (p_BH = .1444)", so the direction is suggestive and the sample is 116 tasks with one pair of models. [Xiang et al., arXiv 2607.21656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21656).

Anthropic's own guidance says the same in plain terms: "A reviewer that's asked to find problems will always find *something*. If you fix every finding, you end up with extra abstraction layers and tests for cases that can't happen." It also separates what a fresh context buys from what it does not: "You get parallelism or a fresh pair of eyes out of it, but not a smarter answer." [Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices). Its long-running harness post notes the opposite bias in grading, that "agents reliably skew positive when grading their own work". [Harness design for long-running apps](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps).

Field data on review bots agrees that noise is common. In a study of pull requests reviewed only by code review agents (CRAs), 60.2% of the closed ones fell in the paper's 0 to 30% signal range, and "12 of 13 CRAs exhibit average signal ratios below 60%". [Chowdhury et al., arXiv 2604.03196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03196). In an industrial deployment, 73.8% of automated comments were marked resolved, yet average time to close a pull request rose from 5 hours 52 minutes to 8 hours 20 minutes. [Cihan et al., arXiv 2412.18531](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18531). OpenAI describes tuning its reviewer to "optimize for signal-to-noise first" and accepting "modestly reduced recall". [OpenAI, A Practical Approach to Verifying Code at Scale](https://alignment.openai.com/scaling-code-verification/). Claude Code's local review ties this to effort: at `low` "the review reports the findings it's most confident in, so you see fewer false positives". [Claude Code local code review](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review-local).

## Passing tests is strong evidence of function and weak evidence of acceptability

When good tests exist, the remaining problem is small. TDFlow reports 94.3% on SWE-bench Verified when given human-written tests, with 7 instances of test hacking found in 800 runs. With model-written tests its result is 68.0% in the results table (the introduction says 69.8%). [TDFlow, arXiv 2510.23761](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.23761).

Passing tests still overstates what a maintainer would accept. METR had 4 active maintainers of 3 repositories review 296 AI-generated pull requests: "maintainer merge decisions are about 24 percentage points lower" than the automated grade, and "roughly half of test-passing SWE-bench Verified PRs" would not be merged. The agents were given no chance to respond to review. [METR, 10 March 2026](https://metr.org/notes/2026-03-10-many-swe-bench-passing-prs-would-not-be-merged-into-main/). An earlier METR update on 18 issues found Claude 3.7 Sonnet passed maintainer tests on 38% and that none of 15 manually reviewed pull requests was mergeable as submitted. The gaps were test coverage, documentation and lint or typing, with an average of 42 minutes of human work to fix each. [METR, 12 August 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-08-12-research-update-towards-reconciling-slowdown-with-time-horizons). Separately, 7.8% of SWE-bench patches counted as correct fail the developers' full test suite, and 29.6% of plausible patches behave differently from the reference fix. [Wang et al., arXiv 2503.15223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15223).

Read together: a deterministic check is the cheapest reliable signal of function, and what review adds on top is conformance to the project's standards (coverage, documentation, conventions), which is where the METR rejections concentrate.

## Escalating after a failed check is supported; routing from the task description is not

Starting cheap and escalating when provided tests fail scored 85.0 on HumanEval at $0.27, against 89.6 at $1.93 for GPT-4 alone. That is cheaper by about seven times and 4.6 points lower. [Kapoor et al., arXiv 2407.01502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502).

Sun et al. (October 2026 preprint, each task run once per effort level) tested "laddering", which starts at low effort and raises it when an external check fails. On Terminal-Bench it raised success for all eight models and cut cost for four of them: GPT-5.6-sol 83.0% to 88.6% at 17.4% lower cost, Claude Sonnet 5 76.1% to 83.0% at 8.7% lower, and Kimi K3 73.0% to 84.3% at 30.5% lower. The other four gained success at higher cost. The paper's own limit applies directly to this repo: "Laddering is nonetheless a stopgap rather than a solution. It requires a reliable external correctness check, which many deployments lack, and it pays for a full second execution on every escalation." [Sun et al., arXiv 2610.34491](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34491).

Predicting the right model before work starts is much weaker. On SWE-bench Verified, routers that saw only the issue text scored Route-AUC 0.565 at best, and a router that first watched the cheap model work for one to four turns scored 0.694 to 0.709. The advantage did not hold on a second dataset, where the turn-watching routers scored 0.48 to 0.55 against 0.52 to 0.63 for the baselines. [SWE-Router, arXiv 2607.00053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00053). A preprint on SWE-bench Pro found a routed system solved 159 of 266 tasks against 158 for the best single model at about a fifth of the cost per solve, and then that "A no-router ablation, always the cheapest fixer with the handoff, ties the routed system", so the benefit came from handing a stronger model's exploration to a cheap fixer, not from the routing decision. [Scrouting, arXiv 2608.04804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04804). A strong-weak collaboration preprint reports matching the strong model on issue resolution at 40% lower cost. [arXiv 2505.20182](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20182).

The large savings often quoted for routing come from question answering and classification, not coding: FrugalGPT's "up to 98% cost reduction", and RouteLLM's reductions of over 85% on MT Bench, 45% on MMLU and 35% on GSM8K at 95% of GPT-4's performance. [FrugalGPT, arXiv 2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176), [RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/).

## Effort is a real lever, and the evidence on effort versus tier is thin

Anthropic's effort documentation recommends `low` for "Simpler tasks that need the best speed and lowest costs, such as subagents", and says of the top levels that "Most of the gain in capability is already present at `high`, and above it, gains are uneven and depend on the task." Its advice is to "Run an effort sweep on your own evals". [Effort](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/effort).

The Opus 4.5 launch claimed that at medium effort it "matches Sonnet 4.5's best score on SWE-bench Verified, but uses 76% fewer output tokens". That is a token count and not a dollar cost, and it is the vendor's figure. [Introducing Claude Opus 4.5](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-4-5).

Sun et al. show the cost side. Moving from low to the highest effort multiplied cost by 2.51 and 3.04 for two models on Terminal-Bench and by 4.54 and 5.34 on SWE-Bench Pro, and for one model success fell from 76 to 73 of 89 tasks between the top two levels. Their tables can be read as a stronger tier at low effort beating a weaker tier at maximum effort on Terminal-Bench (76.4% against 70.8%), but the paper does not make that claim, the intervals overlap, and on its SWE-Bench Pro subset of 44 instances the order reverses (the smaller model at maximum effort scored 84.1% against 65.9% to 77.3% for the larger one). No source found settles whether a stronger model at low effort or a weaker model at high effort is the better buy for coding. [Sun et al., arXiv 2610.34491](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34491).

The Holistic Agent Leaderboard, over 21,730 rollouts, found that "in 21 of 36 model-agent-benchmark combinations, increased reasoning effort produces equal or lower accuracy". [HAL, arXiv 2510.11977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11977).

## What this supports for the skills

The following are inferences drawn for this repo, not claims made by the sources:

- Run first-pass reviews as one parallel wave. Fresh reviewers bring independence, and sequential rounds without new information show flat or negative returns (Self-Refine, Song, Huang).
- Cap the fix loop, and have the cap hand the decision back instead of looping. Anthropic's three-round cap is the only measured precedent, and it is one team's data.
- Fix each finding as a class, re-review the diff only, and do not repost earlier findings. These are three of Anthropic's four convergence changes.
- Keep reviewers from rewriting working code on judgment alone, and filter style-only findings. The reviewer false-alarm and regression results (Jin and Chen, Xiang) are the reason.
- Escalate to a stronger model or higher effort when a deterministic check fails, not when the worker reports difficulty. Kaddour and Barkan are the reason. Where no reliable check exists, this rule has nothing to act on, as Sun et al. say.
- Where a stronger opinion is wanted without a failed check, make the consultation a fixed checkpoint (before committing to an approach, before declaring done), as Anthropic's advisor guidance and Cognition both do.
- Name the model for each role explicitly, because Claude Code subagents inherit the session model by default.
- Treat the planner-strong, worker-cheap split as a reasonable default that is not proven for coding. The supporting numbers are vendor single runs, and one preprint points the other way.
- Keep measuring rounds to pass and findings acted on per round in this repo's own runs. None of the outside sources measures this repo's workload.
